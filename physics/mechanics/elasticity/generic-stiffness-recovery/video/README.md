# Explainer video: recovering a material's stiffness from wave speeds

`video.mp4` (about 9 minutes 50 seconds, 1920x1080 at 30 fps, synthetic narration) explains the finding to a viewer who has just finished GCSE maths and physics. It opens with a tap on a grained block and the question: if you knew how fast waves travel in every direction, could you work out what the material is? It builds the pieces from balls and springs: stiffness, why waves only reveal stiffness divided by density, the three wave types, and the lumpy speed surface of the paper's no-symmetry test crystal. It then counts: a 6 by 6 mirror-symmetric table has 21 free numbers, while the three speed summaries are set by 6, 15 and 28 numbers (49 in all), shown with the crystal's real values (straight-up speeds 1.12, 1.42, 1.69; sum formula 7.1x^2 + 6.3y^2 + 6.1z^2 - 2.4xy - 1.1xz + 0.7yz). Counting is not enough: x squared equals 4 has two answers, and two box-symmetric tables (a teaching illustration) have identical speeds. That sets up Conjecture E of de Hoop, Ilmavirta, Lassas and Várilly-Alvarado. The proof idea is shown as bookkeeping with dials: split a twin pair into midpoint M and half-gap D, apply the three tests, check the paper's isotropic witness (products 2 and -2), and watch the key balance (r - 1) + (21 - r) = 20 hold for every r from 6 to 15. Then come the computer's role (two rotation-symmetric special tables, flat slices, exact arithmetic on a 32003-hour clock, checkable certificates), the direction counts (seven never suffice, eleven random ones do, fifteen explicit ones do, eight to ten are open), and an honest status: computer-assisted, eleven key identities checked in Lean but not the whole argument, internal review only, uniqueness without stability (one simulated test: 0.1% speed noise, 45% error in the 22-number fit), and no separation of density.

Every number comes from `../paper.tex` and `../verification/` through `data_prep.py`, which asserts them and writes `data.json`; `storyboard.md` lists the source of each number, the scene plan, the first-time-viewer review and every simplification. To rebuild, run `./build.sh` with `V` set to a Python 3.12 that has manim 0.22, numpy and openai (it runs `data_prep.py`, then `tools/video/make_audio.py`, which only regenerates changed clips, then `tools/video/render.py --quality h --contact`). `./build.sh preview` renders a quick 480p check instead. The full 1080p render takes about an hour on a loaded machine; the speed-surface scene is the slowest. `subtitles.srt` has the captions, `poster.png` a still and `contact.png` a contact sheet. The narration uses a synthetic voice.
