# Explainer video: how many resting states can a small reaction network have?

`video.mp4` (about 9 minutes 56 seconds, 1080p30, synthetic narration) explains the finding to a viewer who has just finished GCSE maths. It opens with two flasks that settle into different resting mixtures and asks how many resting states a network with only a few chemicals and reactions can have, and how complicated its reactions must be to reach that maximum. A water tank shows a resting state as the point where flow in balances flow out, and the running two-chemical example (rates X squared Y over 9 and Y squared, conserved quantity X minus Y equal to 2) turns resting states into crossings of a balance curve, the parabola Y = X squared over 9, with a slice, giving the two clean crossings (3, 1) and (6, 4). The video then shows why pair collisions only ever give straight or monotone balance curves that cross the slice at most once, and why a three-molecule input bends the curve enough to cross twice. For three chemicals it builds the network with rate constants 1/306000, 1/79475 and 1/121, shows that every rate equals t to the eighth along the curve (60t cubed, 85t squared, 11t to the fourth), and reduces the slice Y + Z - X = 36 to 11t^4 - 60t^3 + 85t^2 - 36 = (t - 1)(t - 2)(t - 3)(11t + 6), with Descartes' rule of signs capping the count at three. It sketches the recipe for every n (choose the zeros t = 1, ..., n), then states the limits: these are resting states, not stable ones; the slice is unbounded, which Banaji and Feliu showed is unavoidable beyond two chemicals; outputs can have more than three molecules; and the result is mathematics about mass action equations, not a laboratory mixture. It ends by separating the written proof for every n from the exact computer checks of 35 resting states for n = 2 to 8, and notes that external review is still open. Every number shown or spoken is reproduced by `check_numbers.py` from `../verification/cubic-sharpness-certificate.json`. To rebuild, run `python3 check_numbers.py` (needs numpy), then `tools/video/make_audio.py <this folder>` (which only regenerates changed clips) and `tools/video/render.py <this folder> --quality h --contact` with the collection's video environment. The full 1080p render takes about 55 minutes.
